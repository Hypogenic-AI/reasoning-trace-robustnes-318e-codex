\subsection{Main Results: Non-Monotonic Length--Accuracy Relationship}
\label{sec:main_results}

\Tabref{tab:main} presents accuracy by policy and dataset. In-distribution (\gsmk) accuracy follows an inverted U-shape: \pnone (28\%) $\rightarrow$ \pshort (90\%) $\rightarrow$ \pmedium (90\%) $\rightarrow$ \plong (80\%). The 10-point drop from \pmedium to \plong confirms that excessive reasoning hurts even in-distribution performance.

    \label{tab:main}
    \resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{%
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lccccc@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Policy} & \textsc{GSM8K} (ID) & \textsc{MATH-500} (OOD-n) & \textsc{ARC-C} (OOD-f) & \textsc{MMLU-STEM} (OOD-f) & \textsc{CSQA} (OOD-f) \\
        \midrule
        \pnone   & 28.0 & 22.0 & 74.0 & 36.0 & \textbf{84.0} \\
        \pshort  & \textbf{90.0} & 46.0 & \textbf{82.0} & 30.0 & 80.0 \\
        \pmedium & \textbf{90.0} & 52.0 & 72.0 & \textbf{36.0} & 66.0 \\
        \plong   & 80.0 & 40.0 & 72.0 & 32.0 & 58.0 \\

    }
\end{table}

On OOD-near data (\mathfive), accuracy peaks with \padaptive (56\%) and drops to 40\% for \plong---a reversal from the assumption that more reasoning improves harder tasks. Among fixed policies, \pmedium (52\%) outperforms \plong (40\%) by 12 percentage points. On OOD-far datasets, the pattern is consistent: \plong underperforms \pshort on \arcc (72\% vs.\ 82\%), \csqa (58\% vs.\ 80\%), and \mmlustem (32\% vs.\ 30\%, within noise).

A notable finding is that \pnone achieves 84\% on \csqa---the highest score for any policy on that dataset. This suggests that for pattern-recognition tasks, reasoning introduces more noise than signal.

\subsection{Token Efficiency}
\label{sec:efficiency}

\Tabref{tab:tokens} shows that token-normalized efficiency strongly favors concise reasoning. The \pnone policy achieves 52.1 correct answers per 1,000 tokens---$70\times$ more efficient than \plong (0.74). Even accounting for accuracy differences, \pshort (7.69) dominates \plong in cost-effectiveness.

Only 4.4\% of items triggered the long-retry path, suggesting the confidence threshold ($0.70$) was too conservative given the model's overconfidence.

\subsection{Statistical Significance}
\label{sec:significance}

Pairwise McNemar tests with Benjamini-Hochberg correction yield the following significant comparisons at $\alpha = 0.05$:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0pt,topsep=0pt]
    \item \pnone vs.\ \pshort: $p < 0.0001$ (\pshort dramatically better)
    \item \pnone vs.\ \pmedium: $p = 0.0007$
    \item \pnone vs.\ \padaptive: $p < 0.0001$
    \item \pshort vs.\ \plong: $p = 0.020$ (\pshort significantly better than \plong)
    \item \pmedium vs.\ \plong: $p = 0.062$ (trend, not significant after correction)
\end{itemize}

The \pshort vs.\ \plong comparison is particularly notable: short reasoning \emph{significantly outperforms} long reasoning ($p = 0.020$), directly contradicting the ``more reasoning is better'' assumption.

\subsection{Calibration Analysis}
\label{sec:calibration}

The \padaptive policy relies on self-reported confidence to decide when to escalate. We find that this confidence is poorly calibrated: mean confidence is 0.924 while accuracy is only 0.644, yielding $\ece = 0.258$ and Brier score $= 0.291$. \Figref{fig:calibration} shows the reliability diagram. The model is systematically overconfident across all confidence bins, which explains why only 4.4\% of items triggered long-retry---the model rarely reports uncertainty even when it should.

\subsection{Confirmatory Experiment}
\label{sec:confirmatory}

We replicate the core experiment on \mini with 4 policies and 20 questions per dataset (\tabref{tab:confirm}). The non-monotonic pattern is dramatically amplified: OOD-near accuracy collapses from 70\% (\pshort) to 15\% (\plong)---a 55 percentage point gap. ID accuracy remains high (90--95\%) for all \CoT policies, indicating that the damage from verbose reasoning is specific to OOD transfer. This suggests that stronger models may be \emph{more} susceptible to overthinking artifacts.

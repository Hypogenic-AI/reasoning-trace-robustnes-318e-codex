Chain-of-thought reasoning is widely assumed to improve large language model performance, yet the relationship between reasoning trace length and out-of-distribution (OOD) robustness remains poorly understood. We present a controlled empirical study that systematically varies reasoning trace length via five prompt-based policies---\pnone, \pshort, \pmedium, \plong, and an uncertainty-triggered \padaptive policy---while holding the base model, temperature, and evaluation pipeline constant. Across 1,250 evaluations on five benchmarks spanning an in-distribution to OOD gradient, we find a strong non-monotonic relationship: medium-length traces yield the best in-distribution accuracy (90\%), while the \plong policy degrades both in-distribution (80\%) and OOD performance (40\% on \mathfive vs.\ 46\% for \pshort). The \padaptive policy achieves the best OOD-near accuracy (56\%) and the smallest robustness gap (30 percentage points), but is limited by poor confidence calibration ($\ece = 0.258$). Token-normalized efficiency favors concise reasoning by up to $70\times$ over verbose reasoning. A confirmatory experiment on \mini amplifies these effects: OOD-near accuracy collapses from 70\% (\pshort) to 15\% (\plong). Our results challenge the ``more reasoning is better'' assumption and provide actionable guidance for inference-time compute allocation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We present a controlled empirical study demonstrating that reasoning trace length has a non-monotonic relationship with both accuracy and OOD robustness. Across 1,250 evaluations on five benchmarks and two model sizes, we find that short reasoning (1--2 steps) consistently achieves the best or near-best OOD performance while using $9\times$ fewer tokens than verbose reasoning. The \plong policy catastrophically degrades OOD accuracy---by up to 55 percentage points on \mini---directly challenging the widespread assumption that more reasoning is uniformly beneficial.

The \padaptive policy achieves the best OOD-near accuracy (56\%) and smallest robustness gap (30 points), but its potential is limited by poor confidence calibration ($\ece = 0.258$). The clearest practical takeaway is direct: practitioners should avoid maximizing reasoning verbosity. For OOD-robust deployment, short-to-medium reasoning with adaptive fallback is optimal, and token-normalized performance strongly favors concise reasoning by up to $70\times$.

Future work should scale sample sizes for sharper statistical comparisons, extend to additional model families (Claude, Gemini) and task domains (code generation, multi-modal reasoning), and replace self-reported confidence with probe-based uncertainty estimates (logit entropy, learned verifiers) to unlock the full potential of adaptive trace-length control.
